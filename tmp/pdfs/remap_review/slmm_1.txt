Enhancing Skeleton-Based Action Recognition With
Language Descriptions From Pre-Trained
Large Multimodal Models
Tian He
 , Yang Chen
 , Xu Gao, Ling Wang
 , Member , IEEE, Ting Hu,
and Hong Cheng
 , Senior Member , IEEE
Abstract— Skeleton data has become popular in human
action recognition because of its efficacy in capturing human
motion patterns while mitigating the influence of environmental
noise. However, overlooking critical action-related environmental
descriptors presents challenges in distinguishing actions charac-
terized by similar body movements. To address this limitation,
we propose a novel framework that integrates skeleton data with
language descriptions to easily capture essential environmental
information for fine-grained action recognition while maintaining
the robustness of skeleton-based methods. We first develop a
Language Environment Description Generation (LEDG) module
that utilizes the open-world understanding ability of Large
Multimodal Models to generate instance-level action-related
language environment descriptions without the need to train
additional modules. Then, we introduce a Skeleton-supported
Environment Feature Extraction (SEFE) module that leverages
the temporal dependency inherent in skeleton data to extract
key semantic environmental features. Additionally, we propose
an Entropy-based Feature Fusion (EFF) module to dynamically
amalgamate complementary features from both skeleton and lan-
guage domains. Experimental results demonstrate the superiority
of our framework, which can improve the accuracy of existing
skeleton-based action recognition methods and achieve state-
of-the-art performance on four well-established skeleton-based
action recognition benchmarks.
Index Terms— Skeleton-based action recognition, graph
convolutional networks, large multimodal models, modality
fusion.

I. I NTRODUCTION

RECENT years, the recognition of human actions has
emerged as an important issue across various computer
vision fields, including sports analysis [1], interactive enter-
tainments [2], healthcare [3] and video surveillance [4]. Many
action recognition techniques use skeleton data as input
because skeleton data exhibits robustness to background noise,
lighting changes, and viewpoint variations [5], [6], [7]. Given
the inherent representation of human joints as graph nodes
and their connections as graph edges, Graph Convolutional
Network (GCN)-based models are extensively employed to
capture interrelated dependencies between body joints for
skeleton-based action recognition [8], [9], [10], [11].
A skeletal GCN-based action recognition model typically
comprises spatial and temporal modules. Spatial modules
focus on modeling topological relationships of the human
body, while temporal modules aggregate information across
the time dimension. Existing research has primarily focused
on enhancing spatial feature extraction and temporal feature
aggregation capabilities of the GCNs [8], [9], [12], [13],
[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. Although such research has made
significant improvement, the relationship between the human
body’s behavior and the environment it interacts with is
seriously overlooked. Similar body movements may indicate
distinct action categories, such as “brushing teeth”, “drinking
water”, “eating”, “combing hair”, and “listening to the phone”.
These actions share remarkably analogous motions involving
the wrist moving toward the head. It is difficult to improve
the recognition performance of such actions based solely on
skeletal information.
To improve human action recognition, researchers are
exploring the integration of skeleton information with RGB
data. This integration primarily focuses on utilizing skeleton
data to guide the extraction of RGB features [19], [20],
[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. By incorporating skeleton data,
it becomes possible to selectively preserve visual features in
RGB data corresponding to specific body parts, filtering out
irrelevant noise. These multimodal approaches rely heavily on
specialized RGB understanding modules (designed to extract
action-related visual information) and the data used to train
the RGB understanding modules. As a result, their general-
ization ability is limited. Furthermore, these models cannot
